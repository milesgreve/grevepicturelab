Logline

In 1656, a farmer signed a contract to save his three children and left the price blank. Four hundred years later, a retired hotel broker inherits a shuttered lumber-era hotel in Michigan, the only heir as always, and learns that everyone the family paid to leave never left. The contract is still counting.

Tone & method

Slow dread. Paperwork before apparitions. The hotel is the monster. We never show an entity — no faces, no silhouettes, no possessions, no changed voices. Period history arrives as felt documents: contracts, church registers, passenger lists, newspaper pages. The target is seat-clutching, not walkout.

Rule set

1 · The Bargain

In 1656, to keep his three children, Thomas Greve signs a contract and leaves the price blank. It fills itself: Einer — one. One heir stays and takes everything. All the others must weichen — yield, give way, leave. The farm survives when the village does not. The price is the plot.

2 · The Name

Those who yield are cut from the name. Men who marry onto another estate take that estate’s name. The adversary collects on the sound, not the spelling: Greve can become Graves and still be owed.

3 · The Count

The house must hold exactly one living Greve. The yielded stand outside and grow by generation. Two living Greves means the count is wrong.

4 · The Ward

A German rite, passed mouth to mouth, keeps them at the threshold. In Manistee it hides in a German weekly — classifieds, church notices, a serial — printed by a judge who disguises it as news. Language is the lock. The loophole that isn’t: Americanizing the family in 1917–18 takes the language out of print, and the lock comes off.

5 · The Counterforce

Submit, then resist. The spoken Word outranks the written contract. The only thing older than the Bargain is what it was made in defiance of.

The horror is the rule, not the dead.

Characters

Ellis Graves, 63

Retired hotel broker. Wants an asset number by Friday. Needs to stop deciding who keeps the property. Short, hotel language; calls dread a liability; when afraid, talks about beams, title, and reopening costs.It’s a closed box. Roof, boiler, a newspaper in the basement. I’ll have a number by Friday.

June Graves

His estranged sister, paid in cash. Dreams of “people” at her door. Needs to be counted, not settled. She never says “ghost.”Don’t tell me I’m dreaming a hallway you’ve just walked into.

The Hotel

A closed lumber-era hotel in Manistee, Michigan. A table set for one. A basement print shop, mid-job, type still set for a German weekly. The body of the Bargain.

All present-day characters are invented. Living people appear only as fictional composites. The Manistee judge is a fictional use of a documented kind of man (immigrant editor, justice, German weekly) — not a private biography.

Story

The others are paid, and sent away.

Prologue — Hohn farmhouse, Artland, 1656

Night. Thomas Greve sits across from an empty stool. The lord will take his children at Martinmas. He writes terms and leaves the price blank. Behind the curtain, his three children’s breathing falls into unison — then one drops out. All three are alive. One breathes without sound.

On the page, the blank fills itself in his own ink: Einer. He signs. At dawn the bailiff rides past the farm without stopping. Thomas stands at the bed and counts. Eins. Zwei. Drei.

SMASH TO TITLE.

Act One — Inheritance

Ellis Graves, 63, inherits the family hotel alone. June gets cash. He reads the clause and doesn’t notice the shape. The hotel is an asset to be valued: registers back to 1892, a boiler, a roof — and in the basement, type still set for a German weekly. The last name on the galley is his sister’s.

Act Two A — A Ledger

The past arrives as documents, one era at a time. Badbergen parish registers whose numbers don’t add up. An 1847 Twistel passenger list: one Greve boards; none arrive. A clerk corrects Greve to Graves. A Manistee judge who prints a Ward disguised as news. A torn page from 1917.

Then a chest of clippings: a Count, the Place de Grève, a poet who faked his death, a geologist drowned on a coal claim. Ellis wrongly decides all Greves are one blood. The truth is worse: the adversary collects on the name.

Act Two B — The Count

June arrives. At 3 a.m. she calls and describes his room before he admits he is standing in it. There are two living Greves in the house. Guest-book entries appear in his hand. He can’t say his own name.

Midpoint: the yielded are every child paid off, married out, dead young. They are the family. The thing keeping them out is the horror — and Ellis has brokered that arrangement his whole life.

Act Three — Yielding

The print shop. Ellis sets the Ward one last time, in German he barely remembers, braided with spoken Scripture — “Submit yourselves therefore to God. Resist the devil, and he will flee from you” (James 4:7). He changes the word that expelled to the word that admits. The door flexes and opens. Nothing is shown. Upstairs, every chair fills with sound.

Disposition

The count is no longer one. He gives the house away. June stands in the doorway — alive, counted, not paid off. No new heir named. No button of music: room tone, then the length of the table, finally long enough. We do not resolve whether that is mercy or whether the dead have simply been let in. That unknown is the scare.

Tragic alternate (in the drawer): the house takes Ellis, June is spared, a new name appears in his handwriting, the press starts another run. Use only if the earned ending goes soft.

Production frame

One hotel and one contained 1656 room. A tiny cast. Period carried by documents and sound rather than crowds and streets. No possession or creature effects. Budget micro-to-lean; the sound design is the VFX budget.

Theme

Every family decides who it counts. ANERBE asks what that costs the people it pays to leave — and leaves the audience counting the people they’ve cut out of their own families.

Budget & finance · the teaching tab

Think like a finance rep.

A buyer or investor reads three things: what it costs (top sheet), who puts money in (capital stack), and who gets money out, in what order (recoupment waterfall). Your hotel-finance instincts transfer: it’s a capital stack with a priority of payments.

(b) Capital stack

Who puts the money in

The capital stack is every source that together covers the budget. Senior (safer, repaid first) sources sit at the bottom of the risk; equity takes the most risk and gets the upside. Hotel analogy: senior debt, mezzanine, sponsor equity — same logic, different collateral (here the collateral is distribution rights and incentives).

Equity — investor cash for ownership of the film’s profits. Highest risk, recoups after debt, usually with a premium.

Soft money / tax incentive — rebates, credits, or grants from a jurisdiction for spending locally. Often cash-flowed by a lender before it is paid out.

Pre-sales / gap — pre-sales are distribution deals signed before the film exists (the contract can be borrowed against); gap is a loan against the estimated value of territories not yet sold. Usually needs packaging.

Deferments — fees cast/crew/vendors agree to take later, out of revenue. They reduce cash needed but sit in the waterfall.

Verify: Michigan’s state film-incentive history is uncertain — programs have been created, ended, and proposed again over the years. Confirm current status, rates, caps, and eligibility with the Michigan film office or a production accountant before counting any soft money from Michigan. Other jurisdictions (for the 1656 room, for example) may be an alternative.
(c) Recoupment waterfall

Who gets paid out, in what order

Money flows down in priority. Each tier fills before anything spills to the next. Common pattern: gross receipts → distribution fees & expenses → sales agent → collection account → debt/gap repaid → equity recoups (often with a 100%–120% total return of principal, i.e. principal plus a ~0–20% premium) → deferments → net profits split (commonly 50/50 investors/producers). Exact order and rates are negotiated deal-by-deal.

Simplified model: fees are applied to receipts at each stage; real deals include cross-collateralization, caps on expenses, and MG offsets. For intuition only.
